# Standing Up for Cornwall

**The CING Manifesto**

---

## 1. Who We Are

The Cornish Independent NonAligned Group is not a political party. We have no leader in Westminster telling us what to think. No central office writing our lines. No donors buying our loyalty. We are independent councillors who answer to the people of Cornwall and nobody else.

Being nonaligned means exactly what it says. When a proposal is good for Cornwall, we back it — regardless of who brought it forward. When it isn't, we say so. Our councillors vote according to their conscience and the needs of their constituents, not according to a party whip. That is a freedom no party politician can honestly claim.

We stood for election because we believe Cornwall deserves better than being an afterthought in someone else's national strategy. We believe the people who live and work here understand this county's problems better than any party headquarters in London. And we believe that honest, independent voices in the council chamber — and in Parliament — can make a real difference.

This manifesto sets out what we stand for. Nobody imposed it from above. It comes from the conversations we have every week with the people we represent — on doorsteps, in parish halls, in our inboxes. These are Cornwall's priorities, and they are ours.

---

## 2. What Cornwall Needs

Cornwall is a county of extraordinary strengths. Its landscape and coastline are among the finest in Europe. Its communities are tight-knit and resilient. Its people are resourceful, independent-minded, and proud of where they come from.

But honesty demands that we acknowledge the problems too.

Average wages in Cornwall sit at roughly 80% of the national figure, yet house prices have been driven far beyond what local earnings can support. Second homes and short-term holiday lets have hollowed out communities that were thriving within living memory. Young people who grew up here — who want to stay here — are leaving because they cannot afford a home or find a career that pays enough to build a life on.

Public services are under severe pressure. GP surgeries are overloaded. Dental provision is in crisis across large parts of the county. Social care, the council's single biggest area of spending, faces growing demand from an aging population and inadequate funding from central government.

Infrastructure has not kept pace. Roads deteriorate faster than they are repaired. Public transport is patchy and unreliable outside the main towns. Broadband and mobile coverage still leave parts of rural Cornwall disconnected.

And through all of this, Cornwall's distinctive identity — its culture, its language, its history — too often gets treated as a brand to sell holidays rather than a living heritage that deserves respect and support.

None of these problems are unsolvable. But they will not be solved by national parties whose attention drifts to Cornwall only when they need votes. They require sustained, focused work by people who understand this county from the inside. That is what CING exists to provide.

---

## 3. Our Principles

Everything in this manifesto flows from six core commitments. These are not slogans. They are the standards by which we expect to be judged.

**Cornwall First.** Every decision we take is judged by one test: is this good for Cornwall? We have no party line to follow and no national agenda to serve. Cornwall's interests come first, last, and always.

**Straight Talking.** We say what we mean. No spin, no jargon, no ducking the hard questions. If we don't know the answer, we'll say so. If the answer is uncomfortable, we'll say that too.

**Local Knowledge.** The people who live here understand Cornwall's problems better than any party headquarters. Policy should be shaped by local experience, not imposed by national templates designed for cities.

**Accountability.** Public money demands public scrutiny. We will push relentlessly for transparent decision-making, open meetings, and proper oversight of every pound the council spends.

**Fairness.** Cornwall deserves its fair share of national funding and a genuine voice in how that money is spent. Fairness also means that the costs and benefits of change are shared across communities, not concentrated where it suits developers or distant policymakers.

**Community.** Strong communities are built by the people in them. Our job is to support, enable, and listen — not to impose top-down plans that ignore what local people actually want.

## Part One: Cornwall Council

The chapters that follow set out our positions on the issues that matter most to the people of Cornwall. These are the areas where Cornwall Council has direct responsibility or significant influence, and where CING councillors will focus their efforts.

---

## 4. Housing

Cornwall's housing crisis is not a statistic. It is the young couple who grew up in Camborne and now cannot afford to rent there. It is the teaching assistant in Newquay who drives forty minutes each way because there is nothing she can afford closer to her school. It is the village where half the houses stand empty for most of the year because they are second homes or holiday lets.

Local people — particularly young families and key workers — are being priced out of the communities where they were raised. The proliferation of second homes and short-term holiday lets has reduced the supply of year-round housing to a level that is simply unsustainable.

**What we will do:**

We will push for strengthened local connection requirements in planning policy, so that new affordable housing genuinely goes to people with roots in the community. We support effective regulation of the short-term lettings market — not a blanket ban, but proper controls that stop the wholesale conversion of family homes into holiday businesses.

New developments must be of high quality and appropriately scaled. We will oppose any major development that is not accompanied by the infrastructure communities need to absorb growth — schools, GP surgeries, roads, and green space. Planning by ambush, where communities discover a major application only when it is too late to influence it, must end.

Cornwall Council itself must take a more active role as a housing enabler. That means working directly with housing associations and community land trusts to bring forward sites and deliver homes that remain affordable in perpetuity — not just for the first buyer, but for every buyer after them.

---

## 5. Cost of Living

The cost of living crisis hits Cornwall harder than almost anywhere else in England. Wages here are among the lowest in the country, yet the cost of housing, food, fuel, and energy is no lower — and in many cases higher — than in places where people earn significantly more.

This is not a problem that Cornwall Council can solve on its own. But it is not powerless either.

**What we will do:**

We will press central government for a fairer funding settlement that recognises the real costs of delivering services in a large, rural, sparsely populated county. Cornwall is consistently shortchanged by funding formulas designed around urban areas, and that must change.

We will push the council to use its procurement power to support local suppliers wherever possible. When hundreds of millions of pounds are spent on goods and services every year, keeping that money circulating within the Cornish economy makes a measurable difference to local businesses and the people they employ.

We will support community energy schemes and press for energy policy that works for rural homes — older properties, off the gas grid, expensive to heat. Fuel poverty in Cornwall is real and growing, and it needs targeted local action, not just national rhetoric.

We will advocate for fair wages across council contracts and services, and for practical support to help key workers — teachers, care staff, NHS workers — afford to live in the communities they serve.

---

## 6. Jobs and the Economy

Cornwall's economy is not one economy. It is a patchwork of small and medium-sized businesses, seasonal tourism, agriculture, fishing, and a growing creative and technology sector. Policy that treats Cornwall as a single entity — or worse, applies urban models to a rural county — will always miss the mark.

The goal is straightforward: sustainable economic growth that benefits the people who live and work here, not just the people who visit.

**What we will do:**

We will push for investment in the infrastructure that businesses need to grow — reliable broadband across the whole county, adequate road maintenance, and town centres that are accessible and attractive. Too many Cornish town centres are struggling, and the council must play an active role in their revival.

We will champion stronger partnerships between the council, further education colleges, and local employers to create clear pathways into well-paid, skilled careers. Young people in Cornwall should not have to leave the county to build a future. That means the right training, linked to real jobs, available locally.

We will support the council using its planning and economic development powers to attract investment that fits Cornwall — not distribution warehouses on greenfield sites, but businesses that draw on Cornwall's strengths in food production, marine industries, creative technology, and renewable energy.

And we will back Cornwall's existing industries. Tourism, farming, and fishing are not relics of the past. They are the backbone of many communities, and they deserve a council that understands their needs and fights their corner.

## 7. Health and Social Care

Health is not a council responsibility in the way that housing or highways are. But adult social care is — and it is by far the largest single area of council spending. The decisions Cornwall Council makes about social care directly affect thousands of vulnerable people and their families.

Beyond social care, the council has significant influence through its partnerships with the NHS, its public health responsibilities, and its role in shaping the conditions — housing, transport, environment — that determine whether people stay healthy in the first place.

**What we will do:**

We will demand adequate government funding for adult social care. Cornwall has an older population than the national average, and demand for care is growing every year. Underfunding social care does not save money — it shifts costs onto the NHS, onto families, and onto care workers who are already stretched to breaking point.

We will press for improved mental health services, with a focus on reducing waiting times and investing in early intervention. Too many people in Cornwall are waiting months for support that could prevent a crisis.

We will champion rural access to healthcare. Not everyone can travel to Truro or Plymouth for an appointment. We need services that reach people where they are — through community health hubs, mobile provision, and better use of digital consultations where appropriate.

We will work with NHS partners to address Cornwall's specific recruitment challenges. Attracting and retaining GPs, dentists, and other health professionals to a rural area requires targeted incentives and creative solutions, not just national workforce plans that assume everyone wants to work in a city.

## 8. Environment

Cornwall's natural environment is one of its greatest assets and its most important responsibilities. The coastline, moorland, rivers, and hedgerows that define this county support rich biodiversity and underpin the tourism economy that so many livelihoods depend on.

Protecting this environment is not optional and it is not in conflict with economic growth. The two depend on each other.

**What we will do:**

We will advocate for robust protection of designated sites and habitats, and hold the council to account on its own environmental commitments. Cornwall Council must lead by example on emissions and waste — not just set targets, but demonstrate measurable progress against them.

Planning decisions must give proper weight to environmental impact. Developers should be held to the highest standards of ecological mitigation and biodiversity net gain, and those conditions must be enforced after permission is granted, not just promised in the application.

We will work with farmers and land managers as partners, not adversaries. They are the stewards of the Cornish landscape. Any transition to more sustainable practices must come with practical, fully costed support — fair funding, clear guidance, and a genuine recognition of the economic realities they face. Imposing green targets without the resources to meet them is not environmental policy. It is abandonment.

We support the development of renewable energy in Cornwall where it is appropriate and where communities have a genuine say. Cornwall's potential in solar, wind, and marine energy is significant, and local communities should benefit directly from developments on their doorstep.

---

## 9. Transport

Reliable, affordable transport is essential to daily life in a county the size of Cornwall. Too many communities are poorly served by public transport, leaving residents dependent on private cars and stranded if they cannot drive. For older people, young people, and anyone without access to a vehicle, poor transport means isolation.

**What we will do:**

We will push for a strategic approach to public transport that connects towns and villages with the services people actually need — not just tourist routes that operate in the summer months and vanish in October. Year-round, reliable services are what communities are asking for, and that is what we will press for.

Community transport schemes deserve greater recognition and funding. They fill gaps that commercial operators will not, and for many rural residents they are the only option. We will push the council to increase support for these essential services.

Road maintenance remains a persistent and justified concern across Cornwall. We will press for adequate funding to address the backlog of repairs and for a preventative approach that fixes problems before they become dangerous and expensive. Patching the same pothole three times costs more than doing the job properly once.

We support investment in active travel infrastructure — safe cycling and walking routes — as a complement to the road network, not a replacement for it. And we will campaign for better rail services and fair pricing, so that travelling to and from Cornwall by train is a realistic option for residents, not just a scenic treat for visitors.

---

## 10. Planning and Development

Planning is where many of Cornwall's biggest tensions come to a head — housing need against community character, economic development against environmental protection, local wishes against national targets. The system should exist to balance these competing demands fairly. Too often, it doesn't.

**What we will do:**

We will fight for planning decisions that serve communities, not developers. That means strengthening neighbourhood plans and ensuring that local people have a genuine, meaningful voice in what gets built and where — not a token consultation after the real decisions have already been made.

We will press for rigorous enforcement of planning conditions. Too many developers secure permission on the basis of commitments they subsequently ignore — affordable housing quotas quietly reduced, landscaping never completed, infrastructure contributions delayed or watered down. Conditions without enforcement are worthless.

We will push for a review of permitted development rights, particularly those that allow the conversion of family homes into second homes or holiday lets without planning permission. Communities must have the tools to protect their housing stock.

The Local Plan must reflect Cornwall's actual needs, not housing targets set in Whitehall by people who have never set foot in the county. We will press for targets that are realistic, evidence-based, and shaped by genuine engagement with the communities that will live with the consequences.

---

## 11. Education and Skills

Cornwall's future depends on whether its young people can build fulfilling lives here. At the moment, too many cannot. The career opportunities, training pathways, and educational choices available in Cornwall do not match what young people need or what employers are asking for. The result is predictable: the most talented and ambitious leave, and the county is poorer for it.

**What we will do:**

We will push for stronger post-16 provision that gives young people real choices — not just the academic route, but high-quality technical and vocational training linked to careers that exist in Cornwall. Apprenticeships, T-levels, and college courses must connect to local employers and local industries.

We will champion stronger partnerships between further education, higher education, and Cornwall's business community. The council has a convening role here, and it should use it. When a tech company in Falmouth cannot find the developers it needs, or a care home in Bude cannot recruit trained staff, the education system is failing the economy and the education system needs to adapt.

We will press for urgent improvement in SEND provision. Waiting times for assessments are unacceptable, and too many families are fighting a system that should be supporting them. Every child in Cornwall deserves access to the education that is right for them, without their parents having to battle for it.

We will advocate for accessible, affordable childcare across the county. For many families, the cost and availability of early years provision is the single biggest barrier to work. This is an economic issue as much as an educational one.

## 12. Culture, Heritage and Cornish Identity

Cornwall is not just a place. It is a distinct culture with its own language, its own history, and its own identity — recognised by the UK government through the Framework Convention for the Protection of National Minorities. That recognition should mean something in practice, not just on paper.

**What we will do:**

We will support the Cornish language, Cornwall's mining heritage, and the local festivals and traditions that bind communities together. Culture is not a luxury or a nice-to-have. It is part of what makes Cornwall's communities strong, distinctive, and worth living in.

We will press for Cornwall's unique status to be reflected in how the county is governed and funded. Cornwall is not just another English county, and policy should acknowledge that reality rather than ignore it.

We will push back against the reduction of Cornish culture to a tourism brand. Cornwall's identity belongs to the people who live here, not to marketing agencies. Supporting culture means investing in it year-round — in schools, in community spaces, and in the organisations that keep it alive — not just printing it on tea towels.

## 13. Governance and Transparency

Cornwall Council spends hundreds of millions of pounds of public money every year. Residents have a right to know how those decisions are made, who makes them, and why. At present, too much of the council's business is conducted in ways that make meaningful public scrutiny difficult.

**What we will do:**

We will advocate for stronger scrutiny committees with genuine power to examine executive decisions, call in proposals, and hold cabinet members to account. Scrutiny should be a check on power, not a rubber stamp.

We will press for council meetings to be fully accessible to the public — both in person and online — and for agendas and reports to be published in good time. Governance by ambush, where councillors and residents discover major proposals at the last minute, undermines trust and produces bad decisions.

We believe the committee system of governance — where decisions are taken collectively by cross-party committees rather than concentrated in a small cabinet — merits serious consideration for Cornwall. The challenges this county faces are too complex and too important to be governed by a handful of councillors behind closed doors. We will push for a proper, open debate on how Cornwall Council is governed.

As independent councillors, we lead by example. We publish how we vote, we explain our reasoning, and we are available to our constituents. We expect every councillor to do the same.

## 14. Community Safety

Community safety is about the everyday concerns that affect people's quality of life — anti-social behaviour, rural crime, drug and alcohol harm, and the confidence that problems will be taken seriously when they are reported.

Policing is not a council responsibility, but the council works with Devon and Cornwall Police, the Police and Crime Commissioner, and other partners through community safety partnerships. These partnerships matter, and they need to reflect what local communities actually experience, not just what national targets measure.

**What we will do:**

We will press for community safety partnerships that respond to local priorities. Rural crime — theft from farms, fly-tipping, wildlife crime, and agricultural vehicle theft — is a serious and growing problem that is often underreported and under-prioritised. We will push for it to be taken seriously.

We will support effective drug and alcohol services. Substance misuse is a factor in a significant proportion of anti-social behaviour, domestic abuse, and demand on emergency services. Prevention and treatment save money as well as lives.

We will advocate for visible, responsive engagement between the council, police, and communities. People need to know that when they report a problem, something will happen. Confidence in the system matters as much as the system itself.

---

## 15. Council Finances

Cornwall Council's budget runs to hundreds of millions of pounds, funded primarily by council tax, business rates, and government grants. How that money is raised, allocated, and spent is a question that affects every resident and every service.

**What we will do:**

We will press relentlessly for a fair funding deal from central government. Cornwall is consistently shortchanged by funding formulas that fail to account for the real costs of delivering services across a large, rural, sparsely populated county. This is not a party political point — it is a factual one, and every Cornwall MP and councillor should be making it.

We will push for transparent budgeting with proper scrutiny at every stage. The budget process should be open, comprehensible, and subject to genuine challenge — not a fait accompli presented to full council for approval.

We will advocate for prudent management of the council's reserves and commercial investments. Public money should not be gambled on speculative ventures. Where the council takes commercial risks, those risks must be clearly understood, properly governed, and reported honestly.

Council tax increases must be justified and proportionate. We recognise that the council faces real cost pressures, but residents facing their own cost of living pressures deserve to know exactly what they are paying for and why increases are necessary.

## Part Two: Cornwall's Voice at Westminster

CING is fielding parliamentary candidates because Cornwall's interests are not being served by national parties at Westminster. A CING MP would go to Parliament with one job: to fight for Cornwall. No party whip. No leadership ambitions. No safe seat complacency. Just Cornwall.

The positions that follow are not a programme for government. We are not a national party and we do not pretend to be. These are the issues where Cornwall needs a distinctive, independent voice in Parliament.

---

## 16. A Fair Deal for Cornwall

Cornwall consistently receives less per head in government funding than comparable areas. The funding formulas that determine how money is distributed across England systematically disadvantage rural, peripheral areas — and Cornwall, as one of the most rural and peripheral, loses out repeatedly.

This is not about asking for special treatment. It is about asking for fairness. Delivering healthcare, education, social care, and transport across a county the size of Cornwall costs more per head than delivering the same services in a compact urban area. Funding formulas should reflect that reality. At present, they don't.

A CING MP would make the case for a fair funding formula at every opportunity — in the chamber, in committees, and in direct engagement with ministers. This is not a partisan issue. It is Cornwall's issue, and it should unite everyone who represents this county.

Beyond funding, a CING MP would fight for sustainable economic growth and improved job opportunities for Cornish people. That means championing investment in Cornwall's infrastructure, backing Cornwall's key industries, and ensuring that national economic policy does not treat the South West as an afterthought.

---

## 17. Immigration

The immigration system in this country is broken. It is unfair to the people trying to use it and unfair to the communities affected by its failures. That much is obvious to almost everyone, regardless of their politics.

CING does not have a party line on immigration numbers. What we do have is a clear position: we want a system that actually works and that enforces existing immigration law at our national borders. That is not a radical demand. It is the basic expectation of a functioning state.

The local impact of immigration policy matters to Cornwall. Housing pressure, demand on public services, and the availability of seasonal labour for agriculture and hospitality are all directly affected by decisions made in Westminster. Cornwall needs an MP who will ensure those local realities are heard in the national debate — clearly and honestly, without pretending that complex problems have simple answers.

We believe in pragmatic reform, not performative and divisive outrage. The country needs an immigration system that is firm, fair, and competently administered. Shouting about the problem while doing nothing to fix the system is not good enough. Equally, pretending the problem doesn't exist is not good enough either. Cornwall deserves representatives who will engage with this issue honestly and constructively.

## 18. Cost of Living

The cost of living crisis is a national problem, but it does not hit every area equally. Low-wage areas like Cornwall are squeezed hardest. When energy bills rise, food prices climb, and mortgage rates increase, the impact is most severe in places where earnings were already stretched thin.

A CING MP would push for energy policy that works for rural homes — older properties, many off the gas grid, expensive to insulate and expensive to heat. National energy strategies designed around modern urban housing stock leave rural Cornwall behind.

More broadly, a CING MP would hold the government of the day to account on the basics that national parties keep promising and failing to deliver: fair wages, affordable housing, and accessible public services. These are not luxuries. They are the foundation of a decent life, and Cornwall has waited long enough for them.

---

## 19. Health

Cornwall faces a healthcare recruitment crisis that national workforce plans have consistently failed to address. There are areas of the county where it is effectively impossible to register with an NHS dentist. GP surgeries are under enormous pressure. The distances involved in reaching specialist care are significantly greater than in most of England.

A CING MP would push for targeted incentives to attract and retain health professionals in underserved rural areas. National recruitment strategies that assume health workers will distribute themselves evenly across the country are not working. Cornwall needs specific, funded solutions — not just warm words about levelling up.

We would also press for genuine parity between mental health and physical health services — in funding and in access, not just in political rhetoric. Mental health waiting times in Cornwall are unacceptable, and a national commitment to parity of esteem is meaningless if the money does not follow.

---

## 20. Devolution

The question of devolution for Cornwall is significant and legitimate. Cornwall has a distinctive identity, recognised national minority status, and a strong case for greater control over its own affairs.

But devolution is also a fundamental constitutional change. It would reshape how Cornwall is governed for generations. A decision of that magnitude must not be made by politicians alone — it must be made by the people of Cornwall through a referendum.

CING's position is clear: we support the principle that Cornwall should have the right to decide its own future governance. We will campaign for a fair, properly informed debate in which all the pros and cons of devolution are explained honestly and thoroughly. And then we will respect the result, whatever it is.

We will not push a predetermined outcome. We will not pretend that devolution is a simple answer to complex problems. And we will not allow the question to be kicked into the long grass by national parties who find it politically inconvenient. The people of Cornwall deserve the chance to decide.

---

## 21. Fishing, Farming and Food Security

Cornwall's fishing and farming communities were promised a better deal after Brexit. Those promises have not been kept. Fishermen face new bureaucracy and restricted access. Farmers face uncertain subsidy transitions and increased regulatory burden. Both industries are under real pressure, and both matter enormously to Cornwall's economy, landscape, and identity.

A CING MP would fight for practical support for Cornwall's primary industries — less red tape, fairer trade deals, and funding that reaches the people who actually work the land and sea, not just the consultants and administrators.

We believe that food security is a matter of national security. A country that cannot feed itself is a country that is vulnerable. Domestic food producers deserve better than being treated as an afterthought in trade negotiations or a line item to be cut when budgets are tight.

Cornwall's fishing ports and farmland are not heritage attractions. They are working industries that employ real people and sustain real communities. A CING MP would make sure Westminster remembers that.

## 22. Our Commitment

This manifesto is a promise and an invitation.

The promise is simple: we will fight for Cornwall. In the council chamber and, if you send us there, in Parliament. We will be honest about what we can achieve and honest when we fall short. We will judge every issue on its merits, vote according to our conscience and your interests, and never put a party's agenda above Cornwall's needs.

The invitation is equally simple: join us.

If you are tired of being taken for granted by national parties who remember Cornwall only at election time, stand with us. If you believe that local knowledge and independent thinking matter more than party loyalty, stand with us. If you are willing to serve your community as a councillor or a candidate — with no whip, no script, and no obligation to anyone except the people who elected you — come and talk to us.

Cornwall deserves representatives who put Cornwall first. That is what CING offers. That is what this manifesto commits us to. And that is what we will be judged on.
